[26]    ABSTRACT:
[27] Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, leading to systemic inflammation, tissue damage, and multi-organ dysfunction. It is a leading cause of in-hospital mortality and accounts for approximately 20% of all global deaths annually. The clinical management of sepsis is highly time-sensitive; every hour of delay in
[28] administering appropriate antibiotics and starting fluid resuscitation increases mortality by up to 7.6%. Thus, early detection and warning systems are crucial in critical care.
[29] In recent years, the widespread adoption of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) has facilitated the development of machine learning-based surveillance tools for real-time sepsis prediction. In ICUs, patients generate massive streams of time-series data, including vital signs (heart rate, temperature, blood pressure), laboratory measurements (lactate, white blood cell count), and clinical notes. Delays in treatment exponentially increase mortality. However, clinical time-series data presents severe challenges, including irregular sampling, extensive missingness, and extreme class imbalance.
[30] This project proposes a robust machine learning and deep learning framework for early sepsis prediction. By leveraging a multimodal data representation that combines tabular electronic health records (EHR) with textual clinical notes (e.g., SepsisDRM, COMPOSER-LLM), the model captures clinical context overlooked by traditional systems. Temporal patterns are modeled using Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) and Kernel Attention Transformers (KA-Transformer) to capture long-range dependencies efficiently. To address missingness and uncertainty, we integrate conformal prediction and active sensing to quantify prediction variance and suggest informative clinical measurements. Finally, post-hoc explainable AI (XAI) techniques like SHAP are integrated to provide transparent, feature-level rationales for predictions, bridging the gap between deep learning complexity and clinical trust. The system will be evaluated on benchmark cohorts like MIMIC-Sepsis to ensure generalizability and robustness under distribution shift.
[32] Keywords: Sepsis Prediction, Explainable AI (XAI), Deep Learning, Temporal Transformer, Conformal Prediction, Multimodal Representation.
[40] [1] INTRODUCTION:
[41] Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, leading to systemic inflammation, tissue damage, and multi-organ dysfunction. It is a leading cause of in-hospital mortality and accounts for approximately 20% of all global deaths annually. The clinical management of sepsis is highly time-sensitive; every hour of delay in administering appropriate antibiotics and starting fluid resuscitation increases mortality by up to 7.6%. Thus, early detection and warning systems are crucial in critical care.
[43] In recent years, the widespread adoption of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) has facilitated the development of machine learning-based surveillance tools for real-time sepsis prediction. In ICUs, patients generate massive streams of time-series data, including vital signs (heart rate, temperature, blood pressure), laboratory measurements (lactate, white blood cell count), and clinical notes. Deep learning models, particularly sequence models like LSTMs and Transformers, show great promise in capturing temporal dependencies. However, translating these models into actual clinical practice remains difficult due to challenges such as data missingness, domain shifts, and the black-box nature of neural networks. This project aims to build a reliable, explainable, and multimodal prediction framework to address these barriers and improve survival rates in intensive care.
[44] Landmark clinical studies such as the Surviving Sepsis Campaign and the Sepsis-3 consensus (Singer et al., 2016) have established that organ dysfunction — measured by the SOFA score — is the cornerstone of sepsis identification. At the same time, recent work by Boussina et al. (2024) demonstrated in a prospective clinical trial that an AI-based sepsis alert system reduced absolute mortality by 1.9%, confirming the real-world clinical value of machine learning surveillance when deployed with appropriate safeguards for false alarm reduction.
[45] [2] PROBLEM STATEMENT:
[46] Existing sepsis prediction systems face several critical challenges that limit their deployment in real-world clinical environments:
[47] High Data Missingness and Imputation Uncertainty: ICU time-series data is sparsely and irregularly sampled. Imputing these missing values introduces uncertainty that propagates to the prediction outputs, leading to false alerts.
[48] Extreme Class Imbalance: Sepsis cases represent a small minority of ICU admissions. Models trained on imbalanced data often suffer from low sensitivity or high false-alarm rates.
[49] Lack of Explainability and Clinical Trust: Complex deep learning architectures (e.g., Transformers, BiLSTMs) behave as black boxes, making clinicians hesitant to trust their alerts without interpretable rationales.
[50] Distribution Shift and Poor Generalizability: Models trained on single-center datasets fail to generalize to other institutions due to differences in demographics, equipment, and clinical practices.
[51] As highlighted by Yin et al. (2024) in SepsisLab, quantifying the uncertainty propagated by missing clinical data imputation is critical for preventing false actions in real-world ICU admissions. Similarly, Shashikumar et al. (2021) demonstrated with the COMPOSER framework that a model which can say 'I don’t know' by outputting 'indeterminate' for low-confidence cases significantly reduces alarm fatigue without sacrificing sensitivity.
[53] [3] OBJECTIVES:
[54] The primary objectives of this project are:
[56] To develop a hybrid deep learning model (integrating Kernel Attention Transformers and TCNs) to predict sepsis onset up to 6–12 hours in advance.
[57] To implement a multimodal embedding model (SepsisDRM) that jointly processes structured tabular EHR data and unstructured clinical text to enhance predictive context.
[58] To integrate conformal prediction (COMPOSER framework) to allow the model to output 'indeterminate' for low-confidence or anomalous patients, reducing false alarms.
[59] To apply post-hoc Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as SHAP and attention visualization, to provide clinical interpretations of model outputs.
[60] To evaluate and optimize the system under distribution shifts across multiple ICU cohorts (MIMIC-IV, eICU) using domain adaptation techniques.
[61] In addition to the core modeling goals, the extended technical objectives of the system focus on robustness, fairness, and clinical integration:
[62] To design an active-sensing policy network capable of calculating feature-level information gain and recommending the single most informative laboratory diagnostic test to order, thereby actively reducing imputation uncertainty during early patient admission hours.
[63] To systematically evaluate and mitigate algorithmic bias across demographic subgroups (age, gender, ethnicity) using fairness metrics to ensure equitable sensitivity across diverse patient populations.
[64] To package the complete data preprocessing, inference, conformal evaluation, and SHAP calculation pipeline into containerized Docker microservices exposed via high-throughput FastAPI REST endpoints for live ICU clinical decision support.
[65] Zhu et al. (2025) demonstrated that the KA-Transformer achieves prediction accuracy of 92.3% at 1-hour, 93.9% at 6-hour, and 96.1% at 12-hour lead times before sepsis onset, establishing a strong benchmark for the temporal modeling component of this project.

[72] [5] PROPOSED SYSTEM:
[73] The proposed system consists of an end-to-end pipeline that integrates multimodal data representation, robust sequence modeling, and clinical explainability:
[74] Data Ingestion and Preprocessing: Collects vital signs, demographics, and lab variables, applying hourly binning and forward-fill imputation. Unstructured clinical notes are encoded using a pre-trained clinical language model.
[75] Multimodal Embedding Module: Implements a Sepsis Data Representation Model (SepsisDRM) that aligns tabular features and textual notes into a shared embedding space.
[76] Temporal Modeling Core: Processes the embeddings through a Kernel Attention Transformer (KA-Transformer) to capture long-range temporal dependencies with reduced parameter complexity.
[77] Uncertainty & Conformal Predictor: Wraps the model output in a conformal prediction framework to identify anomalous or out-of-distribution patients, flagging them as indeterminate to minimize false alarms.
[78] XAI Interpreter: Computes SHAP values and attention weights to generate real-time visual explanations of why a warning was triggered.
[79] The system architecture operates continuously across five synchronized functional stages:
[80] Data Ingestion & Signal Alignment Stage: High-frequency vital streams and intermittent lab callbacks are ingested via Kafka topics, binned into 1-hour temporal windows, and normalized. Binary missingness indicators are attached to explicitly model measurement frequency as a predictive feature.
[81] SepsisDRM Multimodal Feature Fusion: Tabular EHR vectors pass through a 3-layer MLP encoder, while clinical notes pass through Clinical-BERT. Contrastive learning aligns both vectors into a unified 256-dimensional joint embedding space, enabling richer context extraction.
[82] Dual-Stream Sequence Modeling Core: A 1D Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) extracts localized short-term physiological dynamics, while the Kernel Attention Transformer (KA-Transformer) captures long-range temporal context across 24-hour observation windows in parameter-efficient O(T) time.
[84] Conformal Risk & Active Sensing Evaluation: Non-conformity scores construct 95% marginal coverage prediction sets. Patients with high-variance trajectory sets are output as 'indeterminate'. The active-sensing policy ranks unmeasured lab tests by expected variance reduction.
[85] XAI Explanation & Clinician Advisory Interface: Per-patient SHAP values and temporal attention maps are calculated and displayed on an interactive dashboard, providing bedside nurses and physicians with actionable feature rationales.
[86] Liu et al. (2026) validated in SepsisDRM that a dedicated multimodal embedding model combining both structured tabular and unstructured textual clinical data achieves AUC of 0.92–
[87] 0.94 across multiple downstream tasks without task-specific fine-tuning, confirming the strength of the joint embedding approach adopted in this proposed system.

[95] [6.1] FINDINGS IN LITERATURE SURVEY:
[96] Based on the literature survey of 15 papers, the following 15 key findings are summarized:
[97] 1. Uncertainty Propagation (Paper 1): Sepsis prediction models benefit significantly from quantifying the uncertainty propagated by missing clinical data imputation, preventing false actions in real-world ICU admissions.
[98] 2. Observation Window (Paper 2): Early prediction can achieve competitive accuracy using only 20% of the standard ICU monitoring time by capturing weak early temporal signals through multi-view representation.
[99] 3. Reproducible Benchmarking (Paper 3): Establishing large-scale, standardized open benchmarks (like MIMIC-Sepsis) that incorporate clinical treatments (vasopressors, fluids) is essential for fair sequence model comparisons.
[100] 4. Privacy-Preserving Collaboration (Paper 4): Federated learning integrated with medical knowledge graphs and meta-learning enables multi-center collaborative training without exposing sensitive raw patient data.
[102] 5. Biomarker Efficiency (Paper 5): Self-supervised pre-training (e.g., JEPA, VICReg) allows models to achieve high predictive accuracy using 83% fewer biomarkers, reducing diagnostic costs and patient burden.
[103] 6. Responsible AI Framework (Paper 6): Enhancing traditional classifiers (e.g., XGBoost) with SHAP explainability is critical to bridging the gap between raw predictive performance and clinical trust.
[104] 7. Semantic Abstraction (Paper 7): Transforming numerical clinical time-series into natural language text descriptions allows lightweight large language models to outperform traditional sequence models in prediction sensitivity.
[105] 8. Explainable Deep Architectures (Paper 8): Hybrid neural networks like TCN and BiLSTM achieve high performance (ROC-AUC ~0.959) but require post-hoc explainable AI (XAI) to be viable for ICU deployment.
[106] 9. Indeterminate Alerts (Paper 9): Conformal prediction frameworks can identify out-of-distribution or anomalous cases and output 'indeterminate' rather than triggering false alerts.
[107] 10. Clinical Outcome Validation (Paper 10): Real-world clinical implementation of deep learning models shows measurable improvements, including a 1.9% absolute reduction in sepsis mortality.
[108] 11. Unstructured Note Processing (Paper 11): Integrating Large Language Models with structured predictors helps extract critical diagnostic context from unstructured clinical notes to resolve sepsis mimics.
[109] 12. Distribution Shift Resilience (Paper 12): Standard model fine-tuning consistently underperforms under external ICU distribution shifts; retraining or supervised domain adaptation is superior.
[110] 13. Multimodal Representation (Paper 13): Dedicated joint embedding models (like SepsisDRM) capture richer patient states by aligning both tabular variables and textual notes in a shared space.
[111] 14. Parameter Optimization (Paper 14): Using kernel attention mechanisms in Transformers (KA-Transformer) significantly reduces model parameters and helps handle limited training samples.
[112] 15. Heterogeneous Data Fusion (Paper 15): Hybrid models combining Convolutional Neural Networks and Vision Transformers (CNN-ViT) show robust performance when processing heterogeneous structured ICU data.
[113] [7] METHODOLOGY:
[114] The proposed system methodology comprises the following sequential stages:
[115] 1. Data Acquisition & Cohort Selection: Extracting patient trajectories from the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases based on Sepsis-3 criteria (suspected infection and acute increase in SOFA score).
[117] 2. Preprocessing & Feature Engineering: Resampling time-series data into 1-hour bins, handling missingness using forward-fill and median imputation, and performing min-max normalization.
[118] 3. Multimodal Representation: Pre-training an embedding encoder (SepsisDRM) on paired tabular EHR variables and clinical note text.
[119] 4. Model Architecture Design: Designing a KA-Transformer model integrated with a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) to capture local and long-range sequential patterns.
[120] 5. Conformal Uncertainty Layer: Applying conformal prediction to estimate high-variance data points and flag low-confidence predictions as indeterminate.
[121] 6. XAI Integration: Computing SHAP explanation values and projecting attention maps to explain outputs.
[122] 7. Validation and Domain Adaptation: Evaluating across external ICU data (eICU) and applying domain adaptation strategies.
[123] Detailed breakdown of each stage in the operational methodology pipeline:
[124] Stage 1: Cohort Extraction & Sepsis-3 Gold Standard Labeling
[125] Patient trajectories are extracted from the MIMIC-IV (v2.2, ~35,000 ICU admissions) and eICU Collaborative Research Database (~200,000 patient stays across 166 US hospitals). Sepsis onset (t_sepsis) is identified strictly per Sepsis-3 criteria: suspected infection (paired blood culture + antibiotic orders) accompanied by an acute increase in SOFA score of ≥2 points within a [−24h,
[126] +12h] window around the first antibiotic dose. Adult patients (age ≥18) with ICU stays ≥8 hours are included. Control trajectories are extracted from non-septic ICU stays matched by age and admission type.
[127] Stage 2: Time-Series Resampling, Hourly Binning, and Imputation
[128] Continuous physiological streams are resampled into 1-hour non-overlapping temporal bins. For each 1-hour window, vital signs (HR, MAP, SpO2, Temp, RR, SBP, DBP) are aggregated as mean values; laboratory diagnostics (lactate, WBC, creatinine, bilirubin, BUN, platelets, INR) are taken as the latest recorded measurement. Short missing gaps (<6 hours) are imputed via forward-fill; remaining missing values via global median per variable. Min-max normalization scales all continuous features to [0, 1]. A binary missingness indicator feature is appended for each variable, preserving the informative signal of measurement absence. Rolling temporal features (6-hour window mean, std, trend) are computed for vital signs.
[129] Stage 3: Multimodal Data Representation (SepsisDRM)
[130] Tabular EHR feature vectors are encoded through a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to generate dense tabular embeddings h_tab ∈ ℝ¹²⁸. Concurrently, unstructured clinical text notes recorded during the corresponding window are processed through a Clinical-BERT transformer encoder to extract textual embeddings h_text ∈ ℝ¹²⁸. A contrastive alignment loss L_align projects h_tab and h_text into a shared 256-dimensional multimodal patient embedding space z_t, enabling cross-modal learning without requiring paired data at inference time.
[132] Stage 4: Temporal Core (KA-Transformer & TCN)
[133] The sequence of multimodal embeddings Z = [z_1, z_2, …, z_T] is processed through a dual temporal network. A 1D Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) with dilated causal convolutions captures short-term localized physiological dynamics. A Kernel Attention Transformer (KA-Transformer) computes global temporal self-attention across the full sequence using randomized kernel feature maps, reducing attention computational complexity from O(T²) to O(T). The dual-stream output is concatenated and passed through a sigmoid classification head to produce per-timestep sepsis risk probability p_t.
[134] Stage 5: Conformal Uncertainty Layer
[135] The risk output probability p_t is passed through a conformal prediction calibration layer. Using a non-conformity score (1 − p_t for sepsis, p_t for non-sepsis) derived from a held-out calibration set, the engine constructs a distribution-free prediction set C(x) at significance level α=0.05 (95% marginal coverage). If the prediction set contains both septic and non-septic labels (high uncertainty span), the system flags the output as 'indeterminate', preventing overconfident false alarms. The active sensing module ranks unmeasured laboratory tests by expected reduction in prediction variance ΔVar, recommending the top diagnostic test to clinicians.
[136] Stage 6: XAI Integration
[137] To explain high-risk alerts, Kernel SHAP is applied to compute exact Shapley value feature attributions φ_i for every input variable. Features are ranked by positive marginal contribution to the risk score. In parallel, self-attention weight vectors from the KA-Transformer are extracted and projected onto the patient timeline, displaying the critical historical hours driving the alert. A SHAP waterfall chart and attention heatmap are rendered per patient on the clinician dashboard within 200ms of alert generation.
[138] Stage 7: Validation & Domain Adaptation
[139] Models trained on MIMIC-IV are evaluated directly on eICU (no retraining) to measure baseline AUROC drop under distribution shift. Supervised domain adaptation using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) alignment on latent feature representations is applied using a small target-site labeled cohort (≤2,000 stays) to recalibrate feature distributions and restore diagnostic sensitivity. Performance is reported using AUROC, AUPRC, F1-score, normalized utility score (PhysioNet metric), and sensitivity/specificity at operating thresholds determined by Youden's Index.
[140] Tan et al. (2025) demonstrated in MEET-Sepsis that multi-view temporal representation can reduce the required ICU observation window by 80% while maintaining competitive prediction accuracy, which directly informs Stage 2 of this methodology. Furthermore, Tranchellini et al. (2026) confirmed across three major multi-center datasets that supervised domain adaptation — rather than standard fine-tuning — is the most reliable strategy for maintaining model performance under cross-institutional distribution shift, validating Stage 7.

[157] [9] SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE:
[158] The system architecture consists of three layers:
[159] Data Layer: Handles extraction from ICU databases (MIMIC-IV, eICU) and applies real-time data binning, imputation, and textual extraction.
[160] Core Modeling Layer: Comprises the Multimodal Embedder (SepsisDRM), the sequence modeling network (KA-Transformer & TCN), and the Conformal Predictor.
[161] Presentation & XAI Layer: Clinician-facing dashboard displaying prediction alerts, uncertainty intervals, and SHAP feature importance charts.
[162] The Data Layer ingests HL7-FHIR compliant event streams in real time via a message broker, applies per-variable min-max normalization using training-set statistics, and maintains a rolling 24-hour patient data window in a Redis cache for low-latency feature retrieval. The Core Modeling Layer processes each 1-hour update through the SepsisDRM multimodal encoder —
[164] fusing a 3-layer Tabular MLP for structured vitals and lab values with a Clinical-BERT encoder for concurrent nursing notes — projecting both into a shared 256-dimensional embedding space. The KA-Transformer then applies kernel attention across the 24-hour embedding sequence in O(T) time, producing a per-timestep sepsis risk score. The Conformal Predictor wraps this score and flags any prediction whose non-conformity set spans both classes as 'indeterminate', while the active sensing module recommends the highest-information-gain unmeasured lab test. The Presentation & XAI Layer renders all outputs — risk trends, confidence intervals, SHAP waterfall charts, and alert history — on a React.js dashboard accessible to bedside clinicians and ICU charge nurses. The entire system is containerised using Docker and horizontally scalable via Kubernetes to support up to 500 concurrent ICU beds.
[165] Chang et al. (2026) demonstrated in their federated KG-Transformer architecture that decoupling the data, modeling, and presentation layers enables faster hospital-site adaptation via meta-learning without retraining the full pipeline, a design principle directly adopted in this three-layer architecture.
